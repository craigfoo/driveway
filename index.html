Why do it together

One pour, shared fairly

  • Cheaper per household. One contractor mobilizing once for the full length costs less than separate jobs.
  • No more grading and washouts. No regrading after heavy rain, no puddles, no ruts at the junctions.
  • Cleaner cars and homes. Far less sand and dust coming in on tires and shoes.
  • Better access. A firm surface for delivery trucks, emergency vehicles, strollers and bikes.
  • Property value. A paved shared drive reads as finished and well kept to buyers.

Who owns the road

The shared road is two 15 ft strips side by side. 1830 owns the north strip and 1922 owns the south strip. 1820 and 1930 each have a recorded easement to drive over the first 200 ft from Hempel Ave, across the full 30 ft. Cost split

Who pays for each stretch

The first 200 ft from Hempel Ave is the 1981 easement, described as a common driveway for four parcels (see Who owns the road). All four households split those 200 ft evenly, and 1830 and 1922 split the remaining 135 ft to the end at 335 ft. Each household's share is the sum of its stretches.

Rough cost estimate

These are rough estimates, not quotes. Real prices will come from three contractor quotes if everyone is interested. $11 per sq ft is the middle of the $10 to $13 Orlando range for 4 inch, fiber reinforced, broom finish concrete. Fixed costs cover things that are paid once per job: mobilization, permit, equipment and a pump truck if needed.

After the pour

Keeping it maintained

A starting framework for a shared driveway maintenance agreement. Recorded with Orange County, it stays with each house if it sells, so the next owner is bound by the same terms.

Shared sections

Repairs are split the same way as the pour. All four share the 200 ft easement, and 1830 and 1922 share the rest.

Private sections

Each household maintains its own driveway past the point where it leaves the shared road.

Routine upkeep

Sweeping, edging and trimming overhanging branches. Each household handles what borders its own lot, with no cost sharing.

Damage you cause, you fix

If a household's dumpster, moving truck or contractor cracks a slab, that household pays for the repair.

Deciding on repairs

Repairs under an agreed amount, such as $1,000, can go ahead. Larger ones need approval from households holding more than half the shares. Emergency fixes can be made right away.

Paying for it

One neighbor coordinates quotes and invoices. Everyone pays their share within 30 days. An optional reserve of $100 to $200 per household a year avoids collecting each time.

Tree roots

The live oaks along the road will eventually lift slabs. We should agree now whether root damage is shared or falls on the tree's owner, and ask bidders about root barriers.

The apron at Hempel

The section in the county right of way needs a county permit and is maintained by the owners, not the county. It's at the start of the easement, so all four share it.

Disagreements

Try mediation before anything else.

This is a framework to discuss, not legal advice. The 1981 easements for 1820 and 1930 already include upkeep terms for the first 200 ft (see Who owns the road), and this split builds on them.

How we get there

Next steps

  1. Agree in principle

    Confirm all four households are interested in paving the shared road.

  2. Collect quotes

    Get at least three concrete contractor bids for the same scope and thickness.

  3. Settle the split

    Agree on the percentages and the maintenance terms, and record both in one agreement.

  4. Schedule the pour

    Pick a contractor and a date, and plan parking and access for the cure period.
